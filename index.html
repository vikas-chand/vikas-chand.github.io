High-energy astrophysics

Gamma-ray bursts, from the first flash to the afterglow.

I use the light from gamma-ray bursts to work out how fast their jets move and what makes them shine. Sometimes the clue I’m looking for is a photon that arrives several minutes after the flash.

About

During my PhD in Mumbai, I worked on a burst that AstroSat caught through the side of the telescope.

It was the CZT Imager’s first day. Swift spotted an explosion 61° from where AstroSat was pointing. Some of its gamma rays had enough energy to pass through the imager’s side shielding. The pattern they left across the detectors helped us trace the burst back to its patch of sky.

We came back to that burst later. On a plot of brightness against time, it looked like a single flash. When we sorted the photons by energy, we found a hint of a second pulse hiding in the first.

Current research

After a magnetar flare.

Fermi Large Area Telescope

Three photons from Sculptor.

A magnetar is the dense remnant of a star with an extraordinarily strong magnetic field. In April 2020, one in the Sculptor galaxy flared for a fraction of a second. Fermi caught three high-energy photons from its direction much later. The first arrived 19 seconds after the flash, the last nearly five minutes after it.

Did other magnetars leave a glow like this? My collaborators and I went back through Fermi’s records for six more suspected giant flares. Sculptor is still the only one of the seven where we can see that glow.

How fast was the jet?

Gamma rays can collide inside a burst and turn into an electron and its antimatter partner. How many rays escape depends on how fast the jet is moving. In GRB 190114C we could see where the supply of high-energy photons fell off.

GRB 190114C paper

Which way did the light vibrate?

Light from a burst can vibrate more in one direction than another. That alignment, called polarization, can tell us about the magnetic field where the light was made. AstroSat lets us check whether the alignment holds as a burst unfolds.

AstroSat paper

When the jet misses Earth

A narrow jet can point away from us. As it slows, its afterglow becomes visible over a wider angle. With Jonathan Granot and Paz Beniamini, I study when a sky survey might catch that fading light.

Publications

Papers on high-energy transients.

  • 2026
    A Search for GeV Emission from Magnetar Giant Flare Candidates with Fermi-LAT

    V. Chand et al. · ApJ 1011, 148

    Nearly five minutes after the Sculptor flare, Fermi caught a high-energy photon from the same direction. We looked for similar afterglows in six other suspected magnetar flares. None turned up, though for three of them Fermi missed the first 100 seconds.

  • 2026
    Fast-cooling Synchrotron Prompt Emission from Internal Shocks in GRB 241030A

    Varun, B.-B. Zhang, X.-H. Zhao, J. Yang, R.-C. Chen & V. Chand · ApJ 998, 90

    With each pulse in the burst’s second episode, the brightest part of the spectrum shifted up in energy and back down. A bend lower in the X-ray spectrum barely moved. That combination points to electrons cooling rapidly in shocks inside the jet.

  • 2026
    Orphan GRB Afterglows — Realistic Predictions and Survey Strategies

    V. Chand, J. Granot & P. Beniamini · Manuscript in preparation

    A survey telescope can see an afterglow even if every gamma-ray detector missed the burst. We’re calculating how often a survey could catch that glow twice, before it fades, by changing how deeply it looks and when it returns. Conference abstract

  • 2023
    Synchrotron and synchrotron self-Compton emission components in GRBs detected at very high energies

    J. C. Joshi, V. Chand & S. Razzaque · Proceedings of the 16th Marcel Grossmann Meeting, 3009–3016

    Ten hours after GRB 180720B, a telescope on the ground could still see high-energy gamma rays. In our model for this burst and GRB 190114C, electrons in the blast wave give off light, then boost some of those photons to much higher energies.

  • 2021
    Magnetar giant flare originating from GRB 200415A: transient GeV emission, time-resolved Ep–Liso correlation and implications

    V. Chand et al. · Research in Astronomy and Astrophysics 21, 236

    What did the star throw into space? We estimated how much energy a cloud of electrons and their antimatter partners could carry away. The afterglow released about 30,000 times that much. In our model, heavier particles could carry the missing energy.

  • 2020
    MAGICal GRB 190114C: Implications of Cutoff in the Spectrum at sub-GeV Energies

    V. Chand et al. · ApJ 903, 9

    Three seconds into the burst, Fermi saw a sharp drop in the number of photons above about 50 MeV. Within a minute, MAGIC was seeing photons thousands of times more energetic. If collisions absorbed the early gamma rays, the missing light tells us how fast the jet was moving.

  • 2020
    GRB 200415A: A Short Gamma-Ray Burst from a Magnetar Giant Flare?

    J. Yang, V. Chand et al. · ApJ 899, 106

    Two neutron stars colliding can make a short flash of gamma rays. Could a single star have made this one? Its position on the sky pointed toward the Sculptor galaxy. At that distance, the energy and shape of the flash fit a giant flare from a magnetar.

  • 2020
    Peculiar Prompt Emission and Afterglow in the H.E.S.S.-detected GRB 190829A

    V. Chand et al. · ApJ 898, 42

    The burst flashed, went quiet for about 40 seconds, then flashed again. The second flash released more energy, even though its individual photons tended to carry less. Later, the X-ray afterglow flared for nearly three hours.

  • 2019
    Prompt Emission Polarimetry of Gamma-Ray Bursts with the AstroSat CZT Imager

    T. Chattopadhyay, S. V. Vadawale, E. Aarthy, N. P. S. Mithun, V. Chand et al. · ApJ 884, 123

    Sometimes a photon scattered from one AstroSat detector pixel into the next. By counting which way those photons scattered, we could tell that light from five of the 11 brightest bursts the imager saw that year tended to vibrate in a preferred direction.

  • 2019
    AstroSat-CZTI Detection of Variable Prompt Emission Polarization in GRB 171010A

    V. Chand et al. · ApJ 874, 70

    Averaged over the whole flash, the light showed little sign of vibrating in a preferred direction. When we looked at the last of three intervals on its own, the most energetic X-rays showed a hint of alignment that the average had obscured.

  • 2018
    Violation of Synchrotron Line of Death by the Highly Polarized GRB 160802A

    V. Chand et al. · ApJ 862, 154

    In most of this burst, Fermi saw fewer low-energy photons than the usual model of electrons spiraling in a magnetic field predicts. AstroSat also found the light was strongly polarized. One way to explain both clues is that we saw a narrow jet near its edge.

  • 2017
    Surprise in simplicity: an unusual spectral evolution of a single pulse GRB 151006A

    R. Basak, S. Iyyani, V. Chand et al. · MNRAS 472, 891–903

    About 20 seconds in, the burst was already fading when its light shifted back toward higher energies. The brightness alone gave no hint of it. A second, harder pulse could have been hiding in the tail of the first.

  • 2016
    AstroSat CZT Imager Observations of GRB 151006A: Timing, Spectroscopy, and Polarization Study

    A. R. Rao, V. Chand et al. · ApJ 833, 86

    The burst was 61° from where AstroSat was pointing. Parts of the spacecraft blocked more gamma rays than others, casting a telltale shadow across the detector. We used that pattern to narrow down where the flash came from to within a few degrees.

  • 2016
    Radio upper limit on the GRB 161011A with the GMRT

    A. J. Nayana, V. Chand, P. Chandra & A. R. Rao · GCN Circular 20105

    Ten days after the burst, we pointed India’s Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope at the same spot. The sky there looked quiet. At 1.39 GHz, any lingering radio glow was fainter than our detection limit of 120 microjanskys.
